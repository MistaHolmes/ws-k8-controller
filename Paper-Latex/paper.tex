\begin{abstract}
Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) scales containerized workloads based on CPU utilization, a strategy that works well for stateless, request-driven microservices. However, modern cloud-native architectures increasingly rely on \textbf{stateful, persistent-connection protocols} such as WebSocket, where workload state resides in long-lived network connections rather than transient CPU consumption. This paper presents a systematic empirical investigation demonstrating that CPU-based HPA is fundamentally unsuitable for WebSocket workloads. Through a progressive chain of five experiments, we show that HPA (i) over-provisions under cyclic load, hitting \texttt{maxReplicas} within 99 seconds; (ii) induces reconnection storms of up to 1,400 connections per second during scale-down events; and (iii) permanently destroys live idle connections in a staircase pattern synchronized with scale-down decisions. To address these failures, we design and implement a custom Kubernetes controller --- the \texttt{StatefulAutoscaler} --- that scales exclusively on \texttt{sum(active\_connections)} queried from Prometheus and incorporates a sliding-window stabilization mechanism to prevent premature scale-down during transient connection drops. Experimental evaluation demonstrates that the custom controller scales to exactly \texttt{ceil(connections/target)} replicas with zero over-provisioning, holds all pods warm through a 90-second connection gap via its stabilization window, and achieves zero connection loss during any scaling event. These results validate that connection-aware autoscaling with restorm stabilization is both necessary and sufficient for managing stateful WebSocket deployments in Kubernetes.

\keywords{Kubernetes \and Horizontal Pod Autoscaler \and WebSocket \and Stateful Workloads \and Custom Controller \and Connection-Aware Scaling \and Prometheus}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Kubernetes has become the de facto orchestration platform for containerized applications, providing declarative workload management, service discovery, and automated scaling~\cite{burns2016borg,verma2015large}. A central component of its elasticity model is the \textbf{Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA)}, which dynamically adjusts replica counts based on observed resource metrics --- predominantly CPU utilization~\cite{k8s_hpa_docs}. For stateless, request-driven workloads (e.g., HTTP APIs), where CPU consumption correlates directly with incoming request volume, this proportional control strategy performs predictably and converges reliably~\cite{casalicchio2019auto}.

However, the landscape of modern cloud-native applications is shifting toward \textbf{stateful, persistent-connection protocols}. Real-time collaboration tools, IoT telemetry pipelines, financial trading platforms, multiplayer games, and chat systems increasingly rely on WebSocket~\cite{websocket_rfc} --- a full-duplex, long-lived TCP protocol where connections persist for minutes to hours. In such workloads, the fundamental unit of ``state'' is not a CPU cycle or an HTTP request, but an \textit{active network connection} maintained between client and server.

This distinction introduces a critical architectural mismatch: \textbf{CPU utilization does not encode connection state}. A WebSocket server holding 800 idle connections may consume near-zero CPU, yet terminating a single pod destroys all connections it hosts, forcing clients into reconnection storms. Conversely, a brief CPU spike during connection establishment does not reflect sustained workload demands. Despite this mismatch, Kubernetes provides no built-in mechanism for connection-aware scaling, and the default HPA remains the recommended autoscaling solution for all workload types.

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Empirical evidence chain.} Through five progressive experiments (A, B1, B2-Instrumented, B3), we systematically demonstrate that CPU-based HPA fails for WebSocket workloads --- from over-provisioning under cyclic load, through quantified reconnection storms ($\sim$1,400 conn/s), to permanent staircase destruction of live idle connections.

\item \textbf{Custom controller design.} We present the \texttt{StatefulAutoscaler}, a Kubernetes custom controller built with the Kubebuilder~\cite{kubebuilder_docs} SDK that scales exclusively on \texttt{sum(active\_connections)} and implements a sliding-window stabilization mechanism (\texttt{ScaleDownCooldownSeconds}) to absorb transient connection drops without premature scale-down.

\item \textbf{Experimental validation.} We validate the custom controller (Experiment C) under a two-cycle restorm simulation, demonstrating zero connection loss, exact replica targeting ($\texttt{ceil}(800/100) = 8$), and successful pod preservation through a 90-second connection gap.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys related work. Section~\ref{sec:background} describes the Kubernetes autoscaling pipelines evaluated as baselines. Section~\ref{sec:problem} presents the progressive failure evidence. Section~\ref{sec:controller} details the custom controller design. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} presents experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} addresses limitations and future work. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Kubernetes Autoscaling}

The Kubernetes HPA implements a discrete-time proportional controller that evaluates metrics at a fixed synchronization interval (default: 15 seconds) and computes desired replicas using:
\begin{equation}
\textit{desired} = \left\lceil \textit{current} \times \frac{\textit{currentMetric}}{\textit{targetMetric}} \right\rceil
\label{eq:hpa}
\end{equation}

This formulation produces stepwise, reactive scaling that has been extensively studied in the context of stateless workloads~\cite{casalicchio2019auto,lorido2014auto}. Casalicchio and Perciballi~\cite{casalicchio2019auto} analyzed the impact of relative versus absolute metrics on container auto-scaling and showed that metric selection directly influences scaling stability. However, their work focused on CPU and memory metrics for HTTP workloads.

\subsection{Custom and Application-Level Metrics}

Kubernetes supports custom metrics via the Prometheus Adapter~\cite{prometheus_adapter}, which enables HPA to scale on application-defined signals such as request rate or queue depth. Prior work has explored HTTP-rate-based scaling~\cite{qu2018auto} and hybrid strategies combining CPU with traffic signals~\cite{rossi2020horizontal}. However, these approaches target request-response protocols and do not address the unique challenges of persistent-connection workloads where connections survive across request boundaries.

\subsection{Machine Learning and Predictive Scaling}

Recent work has applied reinforcement learning~\cite{rossi2020horizontal,imdoukh2020machine} and machine learning~\cite{toka2021machine,zhong2020machine} to container autoscaling, aiming to predict workload trends and scale proactively. While promising, these approaches introduce model training complexity, lack interpretability, and have not been validated against stateful WebSocket workloads where the scaling signal is fundamentally different from CPU or request rate.

\subsection{Gap Addressed}

To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has (i) empirically quantified the failure modes of CPU-based HPA for persistent WebSocket connections, (ii) measured reconnection storm magnitude as a function of scale-down events, or (iii) designed and validated a connection-aware Kubernetes controller with stabilization semantics specifically targeting restorm resilience. This paper addresses all three gaps.

\section{Background: Kubernetes Autoscaling Pipelines}
\label{sec:background}
%% ============================================================

Before evaluating WebSocket-specific failures, we established baseline understanding of Kubernetes autoscaling behavior under two metric pipelines. These baselines (conducted as separate preliminary experiments) characterize the intrinsic control dynamics of HPA and inform the design of our custom controller.

\subsection{Native Kubernetes Resource Metrics (KRM)}

In the KRM pipeline, CPU usage flows from kubelets (via cAdvisor) through the Metrics Server to the \texttt{metrics.k8s.io} API, where HPA consumes it:

\begin{center}
\texttt{Pod} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{kubelet} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{Metrics Server} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{HPA}
\end{center}

Our KRM baseline experiment used a CPU-intensive HTTP workload with square-wave load cycles (100s HIGH at 50 req/s, 100s LOW at 2 req/s) on a 4-node Kind cluster~\cite{kind_docs}. Key findings:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Staircase scaling:} Replicas increase in discrete steps aligned with the HPA synchronization cycle, producing visible staircase patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:krm_cpu}).
\item \textbf{CPU overshoot:} Utilization exceeds 200\% of requested CPU during burst onset before replicas converge.
\item \textbf{Actuation delay:} Measurable divergence between \texttt{desiredReplicas} and \texttt{currentReplicas} persists through scheduling and container startup time (Figure~\ref{fig:krm_desired}).
\item \textbf{Stable convergence:} Despite transient deviations, the system remains stable with no oscillatory instability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Prometheus Custom Metrics (PCM)}

The PCM pipeline introduces additional stages: application pods expose metrics to Prometheus, which stores time-series data; the Prometheus Adapter transforms it via PromQL and serves it through the Custom Metrics API:

\begin{center}
\texttt{Pod} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{Prometheus} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{Adapter} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{HPA}
\end{center}

We evaluated three PCM strategies --- CPU via Prometheus (PCM-CPU), HTTP request rate (PCM-H), and hybrid CPU+HTTP (PCM-CH) --- across three Prometheus scrape intervals (60s, 30s, 15s). Key findings:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Scrape interval sensitivity:} Reducing from 60s to 30s significantly improved scale-up latency; the gain from 30s to 15s was marginal (Figure~\ref{fig:pcm_scrape}).
\item \textbf{HTTP as leading indicator:} PCM-H reacted earliest to traffic bursts but caused temporary over-provisioning.
\item \textbf{Hybrid stability:} PCM-CH combined early detection (HTTP) with stabilizing feedback (CPU), reducing oscillation.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{processed-results-websockets/pcm-results/PCM-scraping_period_comparison.png}
\caption{PCM baseline: Impact of Prometheus scrape interval on scaling responsiveness. The 60s interval causes visible staircase delay.}
\label{fig:pcm_scrape}
\end{figure}

These baselines confirm that HPA behaves as a stable proportional controller for \textit{stateless} workloads where CPU correlates with demand. The critical question is: \textbf{what happens when this correlation breaks?}

\section{Problem: HPA Failure for WebSocket Workloads}
\label{sec:problem}
%% ============================================================

This section presents a progressive chain of four experiments that systematically expose the failure of CPU-based HPA when applied to persistent WebSocket workloads. Each experiment builds upon the findings of its predecessor, progressively narrowing the failure mode from qualitative observation to causal proof.

All WebSocket experiments use a common infrastructure: a Kind cluster~\cite{kind_docs} with a custom WebSocket server, 800 persistent client connections, and Kubernetes HPA configured with \texttt{targetCPUUtilization: 60\%}.

\subsection{Experiment A: HPA Baseline Under Monotonic Load}

\textbf{Hypothesis:} Under steady, monotonic WebSocket load where every connection generates CPU work (\texttt{CPU\_WORK=1}), HPA will scale correctly.

\textbf{Setup:} 800 WebSocket clients connect over 300 seconds. Each received ping triggers a CPU spin loop, ensuring CPU $\propto$ connections. HPA configured with \texttt{minReplicas=2}, \texttt{maxReplicas=10}, target CPU 60\%.

\textbf{Results:} HPA scaled smoothly from 2 to 5 replicas as CPU rose proportionally with connections (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_a_replicas}). After load removal, the system waited the full 300-second default stabilization window before scaling down. No oscillation, no reconnection storms.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{processed-results-websockets/experiment-a-hpa/replicas.png}
\caption{Experiment A: Clean, monotonic HPA scaling under steady WebSocket load with CPU $\propto$ connections. Staircase rise (2$\rightarrow$5) followed by stabilization window and clean descent.}
\label{fig:exp_a_replicas}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion:} HPA works correctly \textit{only} when CPU perfectly correlates with connection count --- an idealized condition rarely met in production.


\subsection{Experiment B1: HPA Under Cyclic Load}

\textbf{Hypothesis:} Under cyclic HIGH/LOW load patterns (simulating real-world traffic), HPA will over-provision and exhibit slow recovery.

\textbf{Setup:} Same infrastructure as Experiment A, but with alternating HIGH phases (800 connections, active pinging) and LOW phases (connections held idle, no pinging). \texttt{maxReplicas} increased to 15. Scale-down stabilization reduced to 60 seconds.

\textbf{Results:} During HIGH phases, HPA hit \texttt{maxReplicas=15} within 99 seconds --- 87.5\% more pods than theoretically needed. During LOW phases, scale-down took approximately 650 seconds (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_b1_replicas}). The cyclic CPU spikes accumulated faster than HPA could stabilize, causing the system to oscillate between its ceiling and floor.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{processed-results-websockets/experiment-b1-hpa-churn/replicas.png}
\caption{Experiment B1: HPA reaches \texttt{maxReplicas=15} within 99s under cyclic load. Scale-down takes $\sim$650s. Repeated oscillation between ceiling and floor.}
\label{fig:exp_b1_replicas}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion:} Cyclic workloads cause HPA to saturate at \texttt{maxReplicas}. The system wastes resources during HIGH phases and takes disproportionately long to recover during LOW phases.


\subsection{Experiment B2-Instrumented: Quantifying Reconnection Storms}

\textbf{Hypothesis:} Each HPA scale-down event will trigger measurable reconnection storms exceeding 1,000 connections/second, with connection overshoot above the 800-connection target.

\textbf{Setup:} The WebSocket server was instrumented with Prometheus metrics: \texttt{active\_connections} (gauge) and \texttt{new\_connections\_total} (counter). Prometheus scraping every 15 seconds. Five HIGH/LOW cycles executed.

\textbf{Results:} Every scale-down event triggered a reconnection storm. The peak reconnection rate reached \textbf{$\sim$1,400 conn/s} (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_b2_reconnections}). Connection count overshot from 800 to \textbf{1,215} (51.9\% above target) during the storm as new connections arrived before old state was cleaned up.

\subsection{Experiment B3: Permanent Connection Loss}

\textbf{Hypothesis:} If WebSocket clients do \textit{not} reconnect after being displaced (simulating clients that abandon or fail), HPA scale-down events will cause \textit{permanent, irrecoverable} connection loss.

\textbf{Setup:} Same as B2-Instrumented, but the load generator was configured with \texttt{reconnect: false}. Once a client's connection is severed by pod termination, it does not attempt to reconnect. CPU work is enabled (\texttt{CPU\_WORK=1}) to keep HPA operating, but connections are the metric of interest.

\textbf{Results:} The connection graph (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_b3_connections}) shows a distinctive \textbf{staircase step-down} pattern. Each step corresponds precisely to an HPA scale-down event: when a pod is terminated, all its connections are permanently lost.

\label{fig:exp_b3_connections}
\end{figure}

The combined view (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_b3_combined}) provides the definitive proof: CPU drops to near-zero (connections are idle), which \textit{causes} HPA to scale down, which \textit{destroys} the connections. The replica count and connection count are inversely correlated through HPA's decision logic.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{processed-results-websockets/experiment-b3-hpa-idle-connections/combined.png}
\caption{Experiment B3 combined view: CPU (red) drops near zero $\rightarrow$ HPA scales down (green) $\rightarrow$ connections (blue) are permanently destroyed. This is the causal proof of HPA's architectural failure for stateful workloads.}
\label{fig:exp_b3_combined}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Conclusion:} HPA's reliance on CPU as a scaling signal causes it to actively destroy the very state it should be protecting. This is not a configuration problem; it is an architectural failure.

\section{Proposed Solution: The StatefulAutoscaler Controller}
\label{sec:controller}
%% ============================================================

Based on the failure evidence, we designed and implemented a custom Kubernetes controller that addresses HPA's three fundamental shortcomings for stateful workloads: wrong signal, wrong scale-down semantics, and no restorm protection.

\subsection{Design Principles}

The \texttt{StatefulAutoscaler} controller is built on three architectural principles:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Scale on the right signal.} Query \texttt{sum(active\_connections)} from Prometheus --- the metric that directly encodes workload state for persistent-connection applications.

\item \textbf{Compute replicas from connection density.} Given a target threshold $T$ (e.g., 100 connections per pod):
\begin{equation}
\textit{desired} = \left\lceil \frac{\texttt{sum(active\_connections)}}{T} \right\rceil
\label{eq:stateful}
\end{equation}
This produces exact, proportional scaling without CPU dependency.

\item \textbf{Stabilize scale-down using connection history.} Implement a sliding-window mechanism that prevents premature scale-down during transient connection drops.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Custom Resource Definition}

The controller introduces a Kubernetes CRD: \texttt{StatefulAutoscaler} (API group: \texttt{autoscaling.star.local/v1alpha1}). Table~\ref{tab:crd} summarizes the key spec fields.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{StatefulAutoscaler CRD specification fields.}
\label{tab:crd}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
\midrule
\texttt{targetRef} & Deployment to scale \\
\texttt{minReplicas} & Floor replica count \\
\texttt{maxReplicas} & Ceiling replica count \\
\texttt{targetConnsPerPod} & Connection density target $T$ \\
\texttt{maxScaleUpStep} & Max pods to add per cycle \\
\texttt{maxScaleDownStep} & Max pods to remove per cycle \\
\texttt{scaleDownCooldownSec} & Stabilization window duration \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Reconciliation Loop}

The controller runs a reconciliation loop every 5 seconds. Algorithm~\ref{alg:reconcile} describes the core logic.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{StatefulAutoscaler Reconciliation}
\label{alg:reconcile}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require StatefulAutoscaler CR $S$, Deployment $D$
\State $C \gets$ \Call{QueryPrometheus}{\texttt{sum(active\_connections)}}
\If{$C$ is unavailable}
    \State \textbf{requeue} after 10s \Comment{Safe: no action on missing data}
\EndIf
\State $raw \gets \lceil C \;/\; S.\texttt{targetConnsPerPod} \rceil$
\State $raw \gets \max(S.\texttt{min}, \min(S.\texttt{max}, raw))$ \Comment{Clamp}
\State $W.\text{append}(now, raw)$ \Comment{Add to sliding window}
\State $W.\text{evict}(entries \text{ older than } S.\texttt{cooldown})$
\State $stabilized \gets \max(W)$ \Comment{Max over window}
\State $current \gets D.\texttt{spec.replicas}$
\If{$stabilized > current$}
    \State $target \gets \min(current + S.\texttt{maxScaleUpStep}, stabilized)$
\ElsIf{$stabilized < current$}
    \State $target \gets \max(current - S.\texttt{maxScaleDownStep}, stabilized)$
\Else
    \State $target \gets current$
\EndIf
\State \Call{PatchDeployment}{$D$, $target$}
\State \textbf{requeue} after 5s
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The Stabilization Window}

The stabilization window (line 8--9 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:reconcile}) is the controller's primary mechanism for restorm resilience.

\textbf{Without stabilization:} If all 800 clients disconnect temporarily (network glitch, rollout, load balancer reconfiguration), the controller computes $\lceil 0/100 \rceil = 0 \rightarrow$ clamped to \texttt{minReplicas=2}. When clients reconnect 30 seconds later, the controller must scale from 2 back to 8, recreating the cascade HPA exhibited.

\textbf{With stabilization (120s window):} The controller sees connections drop to 0 and computes $raw=2$, but the sliding window contains entries from the prior 120 seconds showing $desired=8$. The stabilized result is $\max(W) = 8$. All pods are held warm. When clients reconnect, they land on already-provisioned pods seamlessly.

\textbf{This addresses a problem HPA cannot solve at any parameter configuration.} HPA's stabilization window operates on CPU recommendations. Even with a 300-second CPU stabilization window, HPA will scale down idle-but-connected pods because CPU is persistently low.

\section{Experimental Evaluation: Experiment C}
\label{sec:evaluation}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Setup}

Table~\ref{tab:exp_c_setup} summarizes the configuration that maintains maximal comparability with Experiment B3.

\textbf{Why \texttt{CPU\_WORK=0}:} Setting CPU work to zero proves two things: (1) the controller does not need CPU as a signal, and (2) CPU is completely decoupled from scaling decisions.

\subsubsection{StatefulAutoscaler Configuration}

\begin{verbatim}
targetConnectionsPerPod: 100
maxScaleUpStep: 3
maxScaleDownStep: 2
scaleDownCooldownSeconds: 120
minReplicas: 2
maxReplicas: 15
\end{verbatim}

With 800 connections and target 100/pod: expected $\textit{desired} = \lceil 800/100 \rceil = 8$.

\subsubsection{Load Pattern: Two-Cycle Restorm Simulation}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{CYCLE 1} (0--150s): 800 clients ramp up, controller scales to 8.
\item \textbf{DROP 1} (150--240s): All clients deleted. Connections $\rightarrow$ 0. \textit{Critical test: stabilization window must hold replicas at 8.}
\item \textbf{CYCLE 2} (240--390s): 800 new clients connect to warm pods.
\item \textbf{FINAL DROP} (390--570s): Permanent disconnection. Window expires. Controller scales to 2.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Results}

\subsubsection{CYCLE 1: Scale-Up on Connections}

The controller scaled smoothly from 2 to 8 replicas as connections ramped to 800 (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_c_connections}). The scaling trajectory was monotonic: 2$\rightarrow$3$\rightarrow$4$\rightarrow$5$\rightarrow$6$\rightarrow$7$\rightarrow$8. Scale-up completed within $\sim$60 seconds.

\textbf{Critical observation:} The controller held 8 replicas even as CPU dropped to 27m total during the idle phase. This is the exact scenario where HPA (B3) scaled down and destroyed connections.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{CPU is the Wrong Signal}

Experiments B3 and C collectively demonstrate that CPU utilization is an unreliable proxy for workload health in connection-holding applications. In B3, CPU dropped to near-zero while 800 connections remained alive, causing HPA to interpret the workload as idle and terminate pods. In Experiment C, the custom controller operated at \texttt{CPU\_WORK=0} --- near-zero CPU throughout --- yet scaled correctly to 8 replicas based purely on connection count. This proves that the scaling signal, not the scaling algorithm, is the fundamental problem.

\subsection{Stabilization Window Design}

The sliding-window mechanism represents a different philosophy from HPA's CPU-based stabilization. HPA stabilizes the \textit{metric signal} (CPU recommendations), while the StatefulAutoscaler stabilizes \textit{scaling intent} (desired replica count based on connections). This distinction is critical: even if HPA's CPU stabilization window were extended to 300 seconds, it would stabilize a zero-CPU reading --- which still recommends scale-down. The custom controller's window stabilizes a non-zero connection-derived \textit{desired}, which correctly recommends holding pods warm.

\subsection{Step-Size Controls}

The \texttt{maxScaleUpStep=3} and \texttt{maxScaleDownStep=2} parameters produced smooth, monotonic scaling trajectories. In contrast, HPA's default \texttt{Percent} policy (100\%) allowed jumps from 2$\rightarrow$6$\rightarrow$12$\rightarrow$15 within seconds. The step-size limits prevent both overshoot during ramp-up and violent contraction during scale-down, contributing to overall stability.

\subsection{Generalizability}

While validated on WebSocket, the StatefulAutoscaler design generalizes to any workload where the primary unit of state is a persistent connection: gRPC streaming, MQTT broker connections, database connection pools, and SSE (Server-Sent Events) endpoints. The only requirement is that the application exposes a Prometheus gauge counting active connections.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
%% ============================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{No graceful drain.} The \texttt{drain} field exists in the CRD but was not exercised (\texttt{enabled: false}). Production use requires migrating connections off a pod before termination.

\item \textbf{Single Prometheus instance.} If Prometheus fails, the controller falls back to a 10-second requeue with no scaling action. A production controller needs failover or alternative metric sources.

\item \textbf{In-memory stabilization history.} The sliding window is stored in process memory. Controller restart causes history loss, potentially triggering premature scale-down.

\item \textbf{Connection distribution.} The controller sets the global replica count but does not control per-pod connection balance. Uneven distribution could lead to suboptimal scaling decisions.

\item \textbf{Window edge case.} During CYCLE 2, a brief replica dip to 5--6 occurred as old window entries aged out before new connections pushed the desired count back up. This could be addressed with a minimum hold-time after the last scale-up event.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Planned enhancements:} Graceful drain with a \texttt{/drain} endpoint, per-pod metric queries instead of cluster \texttt{sum()}, rate-based proactive scaling using \texttt{new\_connections\_total}, and persistent stabilization state via CR status.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

This paper presented a systematic empirical investigation of Kubernetes HPA behavior for stateful WebSocket workloads and demonstrated that CPU-based autoscaling is architecturally incompatible with persistent-connection protocols. Through a chain of five experiments, we showed that HPA over-provisions under cyclic load ($maxReplicas$ in 99s), induces reconnection storms of $\sim$1,400 conn/s, and permanently destroys live idle connections in a staircase pattern synchronized with scale-down events.

To address these failures, we designed and implemented the \texttt{StatefulAutoscaler} --- a custom Kubernetes controller that scales exclusively on \texttt{sum(active\_connections)} and incorporates a sliding-window stabilization mechanism for restorm resilience. Experimental validation demonstrated:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Exact scaling:} 8 replicas for 800 connections at target 100/pod (0\% waste, vs. HPA's 87.5\%)
\item \textbf{Zero connection loss:} during any scaling event
\item \textbf{Restorm survival:} All 8 pods held warm through a 90-second full disconnection via the stabilization window
\item \textbf{Complete CPU independence:} \texttt{CPU\_WORK=0} throughout, proving the controller operates without any CPU signal
\end{itemize}

These results validate that connection-aware autoscaling with stabilization semantics is both necessary and sufficient for managing stateful WebSocket deployments in Kubernetes. The StatefulAutoscaler represents a practical, production-oriented solution for the growing class of cloud-native applications whose workload state resides in persistent network connections rather than transient CPU consumption.
